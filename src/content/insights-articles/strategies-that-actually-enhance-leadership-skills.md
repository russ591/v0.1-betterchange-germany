bodyHtml: '<p>Leadership development advice tends to cluster around a small set of well-worn suggestions: communicate more, delegate more, lead by example. All true, all fairly useless on their own, because they describe the destination without saying anything about how to get there. What follows is less about what good leadership looks like, and more about what actually builds the capability to practice it.</p><h2>Deliberate exposure to discomfort</h2><p>Leadership capability develops largely through situations that stretch beyond current competence: managing a conflict without a clear resolution, making a call with incomplete information, delivering feedback that''s likely to be received badly. Leaders who seek these situations out, rather than avoiding them until they''re unavoidable, develop the relevant judgment faster than leaders who wait for comfort before acting. This is uncomfortable advice because it''s genuinely uncomfortable to follow, not because it''s wrong.</p><h2>Structured reflection, not just experience</h2><p>Experience alone doesn''t reliably build skill; reflected-on experience does. The difference between a leader with ten years of experience and one with one year of experience repeated ten times often comes down to whether they''ve built a habit of examining what actually happened in a difficult situation, why it went the way it did, and what they''d do differently, versus simply accumulating years while making the same interpretive mistakes each time. A short, honest retrospective after any significant leadership moment, even five minutes, compounds meaningfully over time.</p><h2>Seeking feedback that isn''t filtered by hierarchy</h2><p>The higher someone rises, the less honest feedback they tend to receive, because the social cost of criticizing a superior is real and most people manage that cost by simply not offering it. Leaders serious about development actively work against this: asking direct reports specific, low-stakes questions rather than the useless "any feedback for me?", and treating a critical answer as valuable data rather than as something to defend against in the moment.</p><h2>Building genuine self-awareness, not performed humility</h2><p>There''s a difference between the leadership-book cliché of "know your strengths and weaknesses" and the harder work of actually finding out what they are. Structured tools (360-degree feedback processes, honest conversations with people who''ve worked closely with you across multiple contexts) surface blind spots that self-reflection alone reliably misses, because the nature of a blind spot is that it''s invisible from the inside.</p><h2>Practicing the specific skill, not the general trait</h2><p>"Better communication" isn''t a skill; it''s a category. Better communication in a specific, recurring situation (delivering difficult feedback, disagreeing with a more senior stakeholder, explaining a decision that will be unpopular) is a skill, and it''s practicable in a way the general category isn''t. Leaders who identify the two or three specific recurring situations where their current approach underperforms, and deliberately practice a better approach to those specific situations, improve faster than leaders working on "leadership" in the abstract.</p><p>None of this is exotic. It''s also not what most leadership-skills content actually recommends, which tends to describe the trait rather than the practice that builds it.</p>'
