bodyHtml: '<p>A cross-functional team, by definition, has all the skills needed to deliver a complete piece of value without depending on people outside the team. The definition is simple. Actually building a team that meets it, and keeps meeting it as work evolves, is considerably harder than the definition suggests.</p><h2>Why the concept exists</h2><p>Traditional functional organization (separate design, development, QA, and operations teams) creates handoffs between every stage of delivering value, and each handoff is an opportunity for delay and diffused accountability. A genuinely cross-functional team eliminates these internal handoffs by having every needed skill within the team itself.</p><h2>Where it gets genuinely hard</h2><p><strong>Specialized or scarce skills don''t divide evenly across teams.</strong> An organization with one security specialist can''t simply assign that person fully to every team that occasionally needs their expertise.</p><p><strong>Team members resist genuine breadth of skill development.</strong> True cross-functionality benefits from team members who can flex across multiple skill areas, not just a team containing one of each specialist. Building this breadth is often resisted by individuals who''ve built professional identity around a narrow specialization.</p><p><strong>Organizational structures built around functional reporting lines create friction.</strong> A team member who reports, for career purposes, to a functional manager outside the team faces real tension between the team''s priorities and the manager''s separate expectations.</p><p><strong>Team size can balloon trying to cover every skill.</strong> Attempting to include a dedicated specialist for every conceivable need can produce an unwieldy, oversized team struggling with its own coordination.</p><h2>What actually makes it work</h2><p>Organizations that succeed tend to accept pragmatic compromises: a small number of scarce specialists genuinely shared across teams, sustained investment in T-shaped skill development, and reporting structures realigned to match team structure. The honest goal is "good enough cross-functionality to eliminate most external dependencies," not a theoretically perfect team that never needs anyone outside itself.</p>'
