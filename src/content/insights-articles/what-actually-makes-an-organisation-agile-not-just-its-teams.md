bodyHtml: '<p>An organization can have every development team running textbook Scrum and still not be, in any meaningful sense, an agile organization. Conflating team-level agility with organizational agility is one of the most common reasons agile transformations plateau well below their intended impact.</p><h2>What agile teams alone don''t fix</h2><p>Agile teams operating within a rigid organizational structure (hierarchical approval chains, annual budget cycles, functional silos) inherit all of that structure''s constraints regardless of how well they execute their own ceremonies.</p><h2>What genuinely characterizes an agile organization</h2><p><strong>Decision-making authority distributed close to relevant information</strong>, across the organization''s structure generally, not just within individual teams.</p><p><strong>Funding and governance models that support iterative work</strong>, rather than annual, fixed-scope budget cycles.</p><p><strong>Organizational structure aligned around value delivery</strong>, typically cross-functional teams organized around products, rather than functional departments requiring handoffs.</p><p><strong>Leadership behavior that models agile values</strong> in actual day-to-day decisions, not just official communications.</p><p><strong>Systems and incentives that reward adaptability</strong>, including genuine tolerance for well-intentioned failure that produces useful learning.</p><h2>Why this distinction matters practically</h2><p>Organizations measuring "agile transformation" purely by team-level metrics can look successful while remaining structurally rigid in exactly the ways that prevent real business benefit. Recognizing that organizational agility requires structural, financial, and cultural change is uncomfortable: it implicates senior leadership in a way team-level coaching doesn''t, which is part of why so many transformations quietly stop at the team level.</p>'
