bodyHtml: '<p>"Teamwork is important" is one of those statements nobody disputes and almost nobody acts on with any precision, because it''s stated at a level of generality that doesn''t tell anyone what to actually do differently. The more useful question isn''t whether teamwork matters (obviously it does) but why it''s so much harder to achieve in practice than the concept suggests, and what specifically breaks down when it fails.</p><h2>The coordination cost that''s easy to underestimate</h2><p>Adding people to a team doesn''t add their individual capacity in a simple sum; it adds their capacity minus the growing cost of coordinating with everyone else on the team. This is sometimes called Brooks''s Law in software contexts ("adding manpower to a late software project makes it later"), but the underlying dynamic applies well beyond software: every additional team member adds communication paths, and communication paths grow roughly with the square of team size, not linearly. A team of four has six possible one-to-one communication channels. A team of nine has thirty-six. The coordination overhead of a larger team is a real cost, not a rounding error, and teams that grow without acknowledging this cost tend to get slower, not faster, despite having "more capacity."</p><h2>Trust is the actual bottleneck, not effort</h2><p>Patrick Lencioni''s model of team dysfunction identifies absence of trust as the foundational failure that makes every other dysfunction possible: teams that don''t trust each other avoid productive conflict, which produces false consensus, which produces low commitment, which produces avoided accountability, which produces indifference to collective results. The chain matters because it means teamwork problems that look like a motivation issue, or a communication issue, or a process issue, often trace back to an unaddressed trust deficit that no amount of process fixing will resolve.</p><p>Trust, in this sense, isn''t primarily about people liking each other. It''s about a specific, testable belief: that a teammate''s intentions are good, and that they''ll do what they say. Teams build this through consistent, small, kept commitments over time far more reliably than through team-building exercises designed to build rapport quickly.</p><h2>The social loafing problem, honestly addressed</h2><p>Almost everyone has experienced a team project where effort wasn''t evenly distributed, and the research on social loafing confirms this isn''t a perception problem: individual effort genuinely does decline as group size grows and individual accountability diffuses. The honest fix isn''t appealing to people''s better nature. It''s making individual contribution visible and specifically attributable, so that the diffusion of responsibility that enables loafing doesn''t have room to operate. Teams with clear, individually-owned pieces of work, reviewed individually, see less of this than teams where output is only ever assessed at the team level.</p><h2>What actually makes teamwork work</h2><p>The honest answer is less inspiring than most teamwork content suggests: it requires a small team size relative to the coordination burden of the work, a track record of kept commitments that builds real trust over time, and a structure that keeps individual contribution visible even within collective output. None of these are solved by encouraging people to "work together better." They''re solved by designing the team''s size, structure, and accountability mechanisms so that good teamwork is the natural output of the conditions, rather than something people have to will into existence through effort alone.</p>'
