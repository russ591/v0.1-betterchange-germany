  <p>Last month, a colleague threw up his hands in a meeting. "We've got this great strategy," he said, "but six months in and we can't seem to make it real." I have heard some version of this more times than I can count. The strategy is not the problem. The translation of strategy into execution is the problem — and it is almost always where the gap lives.</p>

  <p>The Strategy Translation Canvas is a visual mapping tool developed to bridge that gap. It works by decomposing strategic objectives into progressively more specific and actionable components, creating a visible chain of logic from the organisation's long-term direction to the work individual teams are doing today.</p>

  <h2>The four levels of the canvas</h2>

  <p><strong>Strategic Vision.</strong> At the top of the canvas sits the organisation's direction: its long-term aspirations, core purpose, and the future state it is working toward. This is the North Star. It should be expressed simply enough that people throughout the organisation understand and remember it, and specifically enough to give real guidance when priorities conflict.</p>

  <p><strong>Strategic Objectives.</strong> The vision is decomposed into three to five strategic objectives — specific, time-bound, and focused on outcomes rather than activities. These are the dimensions of success the organisation is prioritising. Limiting the number is important: if everything is a strategic objective, nothing is.</p>

  <p><strong>Key Results.</strong> For each strategic objective, the canvas identifies the specific, quantitative measures that will indicate success. Key Results are leading and lagging indicators that provide a clear picture of whether the organisation is on track. The distinction between an Objective and a Key Result matters: "Become the most trusted provider in the market" is an Objective. "Achieve an NPS score of 65 by Q3" is a Key Result. Without this specificity, progress is unmeasurable and accountability is unverifiable.</p>

  <p><strong>Strategic Initiatives.</strong> The final level identifies the major initiatives required to achieve the Key Results. Each initiative should have defined scope, required resources, a realistic timeline, and explicit dependencies on other work. This is where strategy becomes programme — where the connection between intention and action is made concrete.</p>

  <h2>How to use it</h2>

  <p>The canvas is most useful as a facilitated conversation rather than a document completed by a single person. Working through the levels with the leadership team surfaces misalignments that are otherwise invisible: disagreements about what the vision actually means, gaps between stated priorities and actual resource allocation, initiatives that exist independently of any clear strategic objective.</p>

  <p>A useful discipline at the objective-setting stage is to apply SMART criteria — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — not as a bureaucratic check but as a genuine test of whether the objective is clear enough to guide decision-making. Objectives that fail this test tend to produce Key Results and initiatives that point in different directions.</p>

  <h2>Making it work over time</h2>

  <p>The canvas is a living document, not a quarterly deliverable. Market conditions change, initiatives produce unexpected results, and the organisation learns things that should influence its priorities. A canvas that is not updated as these things happen quickly becomes misleading rather than useful.</p>

  <p>Scheduling quarterly reviews — brief, structured conversations about what the metrics are showing and what needs to change — keeps the canvas connected to reality. The teams working on individual initiatives should be able to see how their work connects to the Key Results and the Objectives above it. That connection is what turns individual effort into strategic contribution.</p>

  <p>The most common failure mode in strategy execution is not lack of effort or capability — it is lack of connection. People work hard on initiatives that are only loosely related to the outcomes the organisation is trying to achieve, and the strategy that was carefully developed never quite becomes the reality it was meant to describe. The canvas does not solve this problem on its own. But it makes the problem visible, which is the necessary first step toward addressing it.</p>
