bodyHtml: '<p>Consensus is often treated as synonymous with everyone getting a little of what they wanted: a compromise that satisfies nobody fully but that nobody actively objects to either. Genuine consensus is different, and harder: a decision the group actually believes in, arrived at through a process more deliberate than simply averaging out initial preferences.</p><h2>Separate positions from interests</h2><p>People arrive at a discussion with stated positions ("we should use vendor A"), but positions are usually proxies for underlying interests ("we need something reliable and well-supported"). Two people with opposing positions often share more overlapping interests than either realizes, and consensus becomes far more achievable once the conversation moves from defending positions to naming the interests underneath them.</p><h2>Use structured input before open debate</h2><p>Open group discussion tends to be dominated by whoever speaks first and most confidently, which anchors everyone else''s contribution. Gathering each person''s initial view independently (in writing, before group discussion begins) and only then opening the floor, prevents this anchoring and surfaces genuine disagreement that might otherwise stay hidden behind polite agreement.</p><h2>Test proposals against explicit criteria, not just group mood</h2><p>A proposal that "feels right" to a group in the room can still be a poor decision if it doesn''t hold up against explicit criteria: cost, risk, alignment with stated goals. Building genuine consensus means testing candidate decisions against criteria the group agreed on <em>before</em> getting attached to any particular option.</p><h2>Distinguish consent from enthusiasm, and accept consent as sufficient</h2><p>Not every decision needs everyone''s genuine enthusiasm. What''s needed is that nobody has an objection serious enough to actively block moving forward. Explicitly asking "can you live with this, even if it''s not your first choice?" rather than "do you love this?" often reveals that consensus is closer than a group assumed.</p><h2>Make disagreement structurally safe to voice</h2><p>The biggest threat to genuine consensus is silent, unvoiced disagreement: someone who has real reservations but doesn''t feel it''s socially safe to raise them, so the group proceeds under a false impression of unanimity. Explicitly inviting dissent and visibly welcoming the answer is what actually makes consensus durable rather than just apparent.</p><p>Genuine consensus, built this way, takes longer to reach than simply calling for a show of hands. It also tends to produce decisions the group actually implements with commitment, rather than decisions that quietly unravel once the room disperses.</p>'
