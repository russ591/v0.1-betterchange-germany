  <p>The Scaled Agile Framework promises a great deal: faster delivery, improved quality, better cross-team coordination, and cultural transformation at scale. Organisations invest significantly in its implementation — training, tooling, coaching, ceremonies. And then, six months in, the promised improvements have not materialised, the teams are exhausted, and the leadership team is wondering what went wrong.</p>

  <p>What went wrong is usually visible in the behaviour of the leaders who sponsored the transformation.</p>

  <h2>The leadership engagement problem</h2>

  <p>SAFe, like all agile frameworks, depends on a particular kind of leadership engagement. Not the kind where leaders attend the kick-off event and issue a supportive memo — the kind where leaders actively model the behaviour the framework requires. Transparency, psychological safety, genuine decentralisation of decision-making, comfort with uncertainty.</p>

  <p>These are not natural defaults for most people who have risen to senior leadership through traditional organisational structures. Those structures reward the appearance of certainty, the maintenance of control, and clear upward accountability for results. Agile asks leaders to behave in ways that can feel counterintuitive to the point of uncomfortable.</p>

  <p>The resistance that results is rarely explicit. Leaders do not typically say "I don't believe in this." They say "yes, but in our context we need to..." and then describe a slightly modified version of the status quo.</p>

  <h2>What actually helps</h2>

  <p>There are four approaches that consistently improve the quality of leadership engagement in SAFe transformations.</p>

  <p><strong>Education that goes beyond the mechanics.</strong> Leaders who understand not just how SAFe works but why it works — the underlying principles of lean thinking, flow, and systems optimisation — are more capable of making good judgment calls when the framework's prescriptions don't quite fit their context. SAFe at the level of ceremony compliance produces compliance. SAFe at the level of principled understanding produces real change.</p>

  <p><strong>Active participation in agile ceremonies.</strong> Leaders who attend Sprint Reviews, PI Planning events, and retrospectives as genuine participants — who listen to what teams are surfacing rather than simply presenting the strategic context — demonstrate commitment more effectively than any communication. People notice the difference between leaders who are there and leaders who are present.</p>

  <p><strong>Psychological safety as a deliberate creation.</strong> Agile transformation surfaces problems. That is, in fact, the point. Teams that feel safe raising problems surface them early, when they are small and manageable. Teams that do not feel safe suppress them until they become crises. Leaders who respond to surfaced problems with support rather than blame are building the organisational nervous system that agile methods require.</p>

  <p><strong>Aligned incentives.</strong> The most effective signal about what an organisation actually values is what it measures and rewards. If the incentive structures reward traditional delivery metrics — on-time, on-budget, to-spec — while the transformation is asking for agile behaviours, the incentives win. Aligning what is rewarded with what is required is the structural change that makes the cultural change sustainable.</p>

  <h2>The honest assessment</h2>

  <p>SAFe is a sophisticated and well-developed framework. The organisations that get the most from it are not the ones with the best implementation methodology. They are the ones where the leadership team genuinely wants to work differently — and is prepared to demonstrate that in their own behaviour, not just in the teams they are responsible for.</p>

  <p>If you are a leader in an organisation considering SAFe, the most useful question to ask yourself is not "how do we implement this?" It is "am I willing to behave differently in order to model what this requires?" The answer to that question will shape the outcomes more than any other variable.</p>
