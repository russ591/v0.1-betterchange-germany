bodyHtml: '<p>Unstructured coaching conversations tend to drift: a promising opening question leads somewhere interesting, but the conversation ends without a clear sense of what was accomplished. Structure solves this without turning coaching into a rigid script, provided it''s used as scaffolding rather than a substitute for genuine listening.</p><h2>The GROW model, as a widely-used example</h2><p><strong>Goal:</strong> what the person actually wants from this conversation, made explicit rather than assumed. Skipping this is common and costly.</p><p><strong>Reality:</strong> the current situation, explored honestly rather than through the comfortable version. This is where a coach''s questions do real work.</p><p><strong>Options:</strong> genuinely exploring possibilities, without the coach''s own preferred solution silently steering the conversation.</p><p><strong>Will (or Way Forward):</strong> converting the conversation into a genuine commitment, not just a pleasant discussion that ends without a next step.</p><h2>Why structure helps even experienced coaches</h2><p>Structure is useful for noticing, in the moment, when a conversation has drifted: recognising "we''ve spent fifteen minutes in Reality and haven''t touched Options yet" is a form of real-time self-monitoring much harder to maintain without an explicit structure to check against.</p><h2>Where structure becomes a problem</h2><p>Rigidly following a structure regardless of what a conversation actually needs turns useful scaffolding into a script that fights against the conversation''s genuine needs. The skill is holding the structure loosely.</p><h2>The practical takeaway</h2><p>A structure like GROW gives a coach a reliable way to keep conversations purposeful without relying purely on in-the-moment improvisation, useful for less experienced coaches building the habit, and still useful for experienced coaches as a periodic self-check.</p>'
