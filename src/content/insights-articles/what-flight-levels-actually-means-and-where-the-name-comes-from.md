bodyHtml: '<p>Flight Levels comes up constantly in conversations about organisational agility, but for anyone encountering the term for the first time, a genuine definition is more useful than fluent name-dropping. Here is the plain version, starting with where the name itself comes from.</p><h2>Where the name comes from</h2><p>The term is borrowed from aviation. Aircraft in controlled airspace fly at standardised altitude bands, and a pilot''s view of the world changes with altitude: low down, individual obstacles and details are visible; higher up, the picture is broader but coarser. Klaus Leopold, who developed Flight Levels, used this as a deliberate metaphor for organisations: different levels of an organisation genuinely see different things, and none of those views is more "correct" than another, they simply cover different scope.</p><h2>The three levels, briefly</h2><p>Flight Levels describes three layers of activity in an organisation. The <strong>operational layer</strong> is where teams do the work itself: sprints, tickets, day-to-day delivery. The <strong>coordination layer</strong> is where work flows across teams, dependencies get managed, and priorities are set across multiple product areas. The <strong>strategic layer</strong> is where organisational direction is set, and where that direction should connect back to what teams are actually doing. Each layer needs its own way of working; none of them substitutes for the others.</p><h2>The problem it exists to solve</h2><p>Most organisations investing in agility have put the bulk of that investment into the operational layer: team-level Scrum, Kanban boards, sprint ceremonies. That investment often produces genuinely better-functioning teams that are still, collectively, working on the wrong things, in the wrong order, or blocked waiting on decisions made too slowly elsewhere. The teams are healthy; the layers above them are not. Flight Levels exists to name that gap explicitly and give organisations a vocabulary for closing it, rather than continuing to invest exclusively at the level that already got the most attention.</p><h2>Why it''s a thinking model, not a framework to adopt</h2><p>Flight Levels doesn''t prescribe specific ceremonies, roles, or artefacts the way Scrum or SAFe do. It gives a way of diagnosing which layer a real problem actually lives in, and designing a response fitted to that specific organisation, rather than importing a standard playbook. This is a deliberate design choice: two organisations can both apply Flight Levels thinking and end up with genuinely different coordination systems, because the systems are built for their own context rather than copied from a template.</p><h2>Where this fits with the rest of what''s on this site</h2><p>This piece is deliberately just the plain orientation: what the name means, what the three levels are, and what problem the model addresses. For a closer look at why Flight Levels resists being treated as a framework, see the companion article on that distinction; for how the thinking actually translates into practice, see the piece on its five key activities. If applying any of this to a specific organisation is the goal, our FL2D and FL3D workshops are the most direct route in.</p>'
