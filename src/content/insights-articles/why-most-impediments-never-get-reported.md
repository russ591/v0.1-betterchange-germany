bodyHtml: '<p>Scrum assumes impediments get raised, typically in the Daily Scrum. In practice, a substantial share of real impediments never get explicitly raised at all, which makes the discovery problem at least as important as the solving problem.</p><h2>Why impediments go unreported</h2><p><strong>Normalization.</strong> An impediment present for months stops registering as "an impediment" and starts registering as simply "how things are here."</p><p><strong>Perceived unsolvability.</strong> Team members sometimes correctly judge that raising something beyond the Scrum Master''s power to fix isn''t worth the effort.</p><p><strong>Attribution to self rather than system.</strong> A team member struggling with unclear requirements sometimes attributes the difficulty to their own inadequate skill, rather than a legitimate systemic impediment.</p><p><strong>Social cost of raising blame-adjacent impediments.</strong> An impediment caused by another team or individual carries real social risk to name directly.</p><h2>Better discovery mechanisms than waiting for the Daily Scrum</h2><p><strong>Direct observation:</strong> watching for hesitation, frustration, or quiet workarounds, rather than relying entirely on people to proactively announce problems.</p><p><strong>Explicitly asking about normalized friction:</strong> a periodic "what''s become ''how things are'' that''s actually still a real problem?" surfaces impediments that won''t come up through passive listening.</p><p><strong>Making it explicitly safe to name organizationally-caused impediments</strong>, framed as system problems rather than individual failures.</p><h2>Solving what gets found</h2><p>Impediments split into those solvable within the team and those requiring organizational-level intervention. The second category requires the Scrum Master to actually escalate and advocate outside the team, often where the most consequential, longest-standing impediments actually get resolved.</p>'
