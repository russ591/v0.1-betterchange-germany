bodyHtml: '<p>"Self-organizing" is one of the most frequently cited agile principles and one of the most commonly misapplied, largely because it''s often interpreted as "no management structure at all" rather than what it actually means: a team with genuine authority over how it accomplishes its work, operating within boundaries someone else still sets.</p><h2>What self-organization actually covers</h2><p>A self-organizing team decides, internally, how to approach a piece of work, who takes on which parts of it, what technical methods to use, and how to structure its own internal collaboration. These are real, substantive decisions, and a team that genuinely controls them has meaningfully more autonomy than one where a manager assigns individual tasks directly.</p><h2>What self-organization does not cover</h2><p>The team does not typically decide what to work on: that''s the Product Owner''s accountability. It doesn''t set organizational budget, doesn''t determine headcount, and doesn''t operate free of organizational constraints that apply regardless of team autonomy. Self-organization is real authority over "how," bounded by decisions about "what" that remain elsewhere.</p><h2>Why the boundary gets misunderstood</h2><p>Teams new to self-organization sometimes interpret it as license to ignore prioritization decisions they disagree with. This is a genuine misunderstanding of scope, and it tends to produce conflict with the Product Owner mistakenly framed as a fight over "autonomy" when it''s actually a fight over who owns prioritization, a question self-organization was never meant to touch.</p><h2>What has to be true for self-organization to work well</h2><p><strong>Genuine trust from management</strong>, sustained even when the team''s chosen approach isn''t what a manager would have chosen themselves. Self-organization that''s only tolerated when it matches what management would have decided anyway isn''t really self-organization.</p><p><strong>Sufficient shared skill and context</strong> within the team to actually make good decisions about approach without needing to escalate every choice.</p><p><strong>Clarity about the boundaries</strong>, so the team knows confidently which decisions are genuinely theirs. Ambiguity about the boundary is where most friction around self-organization actually originates.</p><p>Self-organization, understood this way, is neither the absence of structure nor a synonym for full autonomy. It''s a specific, bounded delegation of authority over "how," and it works best when both the team and the organization share a clear, explicit understanding of exactly where that boundary sits.</p>'
