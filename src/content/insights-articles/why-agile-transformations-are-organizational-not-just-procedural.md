bodyHtml: '<p>Agile transformation is frequently executed as process change: adopt Scrum, train the teams, run the new ceremonies. This framing consistently under-delivers, because process change addresses only a fraction of what genuine transformation requires.</p><h2>Why the process-only approach falls short</h2><p>A team can run every ceremony correctly and still operate within a structure that undermines everything those ceremonies aim for: rigid annual budgeting, hierarchical approval chains, performance systems rewarding individual output over team outcomes.</p><h2>What actually needs to change, beyond process</h2><p><strong>Organizational structure.</strong> Functional silos create handoffs no amount of good ceremony execution can overcome. Genuine transformation often requires restructuring around cross-functional teams, a far larger change than adopting new meeting formats.</p><p><strong>Funding and governance models.</strong> Annual budget cycles are fundamentally in tension with iterative, adaptive delivery. Keeping traditional funding while adopting agile delivery creates a structural conflict.</p><p><strong>Leadership behavior and culture.</strong> Leaders who continue expecting detailed upfront plans, or who intervene in team-level decisions, actively work against transformation regardless of official documentation.</p><p><strong>Performance management and incentives.</strong> Inherited incentive structures often reward exactly the behaviors agile ways of working are trying to move away from, and unchanged incentives quietly pull behavior in the old direction.</p><h2>Why this makes transformation genuinely difficult</h2><p>Each dimension is harder to change than a meeting cadence and more likely to encounter resistance, because these touch power structures and career incentives people have built their professional identity around. This is why so many transformations plateau at the team level: the parts within a team''s control transform relatively easily; the surrounding organizational structures, requiring senior leadership commitment, often don''t.</p>'
